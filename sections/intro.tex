\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Collections of time series can often be aggregated at several levels by geography, product, or other attributes. Forecasts are usually needed for every series at every level, and they should be coherent: each aggregate forecast should equal the sum of the forecasts below it. Forecast reconciliation achieves this by forecasting every series independently and then adjusting the forecasts to satisfy the aggregation constraints \citep{hyndman2011optimal,athanasopoulos2023forecast}. The minimum trace (MinT) method of \citet{wickramasuriya2019optimal} chooses the adjustment using the covariance matrix of the base forecast errors, and it is now the standard approach.

Reconciliation is almost always applied to one variable at a time, yet many hierarchies carry several variables. Brazil records monthly worker admissions and dismissals for each of its 27 federative units, which aggregate to five regions and to the country. The two variables are driven by common labour market conditions, and their forecast errors are correlated. Because MinT works by exploiting the covariance of the base forecast errors, it seems natural to reconcile both variables together, using a covariance matrix that spans them, and to expect this to beat reconciling each separately. This paper asks when that expectation is correct.

Joint reconciliation of several variables is not the same problem as reconciling a grouped hierarchy. In a grouped hierarchy, such as tourism demand disaggregated by both region and travel purpose, the categories of each grouping variable are linked by aggregation constraints: total tourism is the sum over purposes, and that total is itself forecast. Here nothing sums admissions to dismissals. The stacked summing matrix is block diagonal by variable, and the only link between the variables is the error covariance.

We give an exact answer. MinT can be read as a regression of the base forecast errors on the observed incoherences of the base forecasts. Joint reconciliation of several variables coincides with separate reconciliation if and only if, once each variable has been reconciled on its own, its remaining errors are uncorrelated with the incoherences of every other variable. Two known results are special cases. MinT is unaffected by any component of the error covariance that lies in the coherent subspace \citep{wickramasuriya2021properties}, so cross-variable dependence of that form is invisible. And when the error covariance is separable, a Kronecker product of a cross-variable matrix and a cross-series matrix, joint and separate reconciliation coincide whatever the cross-variable correlation, a special case of a result of \citet{girolimetto2024insights} for cross-temporal reconciliation and the reconciliation analogue of Zellner's observation that seemingly unrelated regressions with identical regressors reduce to equation-by-equation least squares \citep{zellner1962efficient}.

The characterisation has an immediate consequence for how joint reconciliation has been evaluated. Simulation designs typically generate correlated innovations for the bottom-level series of every variable and give every node the same dynamics. We show that the population-optimal forecasts in such designs are coherent, so reconciliation of any kind has nothing to act on, and differences between joint and separate reconciliation arise only from estimation error in the base models. Varying the cross-variable correlation in such a design says nothing about the value of joint reconciliation.

For real data we need to know whether the conditions of the characterisation fail, and by how much it matters. Measures of distance from a Kronecker product answer the wrong question, because they respond to components of the covariance that reconciliation ignores. We propose two effect sizes: $\kappa$, the weight that the joint reconciliation places on other variables' incoherences, and $\gamma$, the reduction in mean squared error that it would deliver if the estimated covariance were correct. We also propose a likelihood ratio test of the characterising condition, which uses the regression form of MinT and needs no simulation.
