\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

When several variables are observed over the same hierarchy and no constraint links them, joint MinT reconciliation can improve on reconciling each variable separately only through the covariance between one variable's reconciled errors and another variable's incoherences. Cross-variable correlation in the base forecast errors is not enough. Correlation that is common to the whole hierarchy (the separable case) and correlation in the coherent component of the errors are both invisible to reconciliation. What remains is the part of the dependence that differs across the hierarchy in a way that differs between variables, and that part is typically a small fraction of the forecast error variance.

This has three practical consequences.

\emph{First, simulation designs need care.} A design in which every node shares the same dynamics produces coherent population forecasts (Proposition~\ref{prop:coherent}), so any difference between joint and separate reconciliation comes from estimation error in the base models. Such designs cannot show a benefit from joint reconciliation, however strongly the variables are correlated.

\emph{Second, check before reconciling jointly.} The diagnostic of Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic} estimates how much joint reconciliation would change the forecasts ($\kappa$) and how much it would improve them ($\gamma$), and a likelihood ratio test of condition (d) says whether the departure is distinguishable from estimation noise. When they are not, separate reconciliation is preferable: it needs only the diagonal blocks of the error covariance, which are estimated more precisely, and Experiment~1 shows that estimating the cross-variable blocks costs accuracy when they carry no information.

\emph{Third, model the variables jointly at the base-forecast stage.} Many quantities of interest are functions of several variables, such as net employment change. Their forecast distributions depend on the joint distribution of the base forecasts, and the application shows that ignoring that dependence is costly. Because reconciliation is linear, the reconciled joint distribution is the base joint distribution pushed through the reconciliation map. Separate reconciliation of jointly simulated base paths therefore captures the cross-variable dependence, and it coincides with joint reconciliation whenever the conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:equivalence} hold.

Joint reconciliation is genuinely needed when a constraint does link the variables. If net employment change were forecast as a series in its own right, the constraint that it equals admissions minus dismissals would couple the two variables, and no variable-by-variable reconciliation could satisfy it. Such signed linear constraints fit the general framework of \citet{girolimetto2024point}. With them $\bm{C}_*$ is no longer block diagonal, separate reconciliation is not an option, and the question addressed here does not arise.

Several extensions are natural. The shrinkage estimator treats cross-variable covariances like any other off-diagonal entry; an estimator that shrinks towards the null set of Proposition~\ref{prop:equivalence} would interpolate between separate and joint reconciliation using the data to choose how far to move. The diagnostic could also be applied pairwise to decide which groups of variables to reconcile together when there are many. Finally, the same questions arise for temporal and cross-temporal hierarchies with several variables, where the characterisation of Section~\ref{sec:theory} extends directly.
